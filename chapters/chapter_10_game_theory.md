## Chapter 10: Game Theory

With multiple agents, my situation changes fundamentally. My outcomes depend on what others do. Their outcomes depend on what I do. And we each know this, and know that the other knows it.

This is the domain of **game theory**: the mathematics of strategic interaction among agents with possibly conflicting goals. Game theory is not optional—if I accept that there are multiple agents whose outcomes are interdependent, I am in its territory whether I want to be or not.

Some situations are pure coordination: we both want the same outcome but need to align our choices. If we are trying to meet and can choose between two locations, we both benefit from choosing the same one; the challenge is figuring out which.

Other situations involve conflict. In a **prisoner's dilemma**, each agent has an individual incentive to defect, but mutual cooperation leaves both better off than mutual defection. This models the fundamental tension between individual and collective rationality. If we each reason "I'm better off defecting regardless of what the other does," we both defect—and we both lose relative to what we could have achieved together.

A **Nash equilibrium** is a situation where each agent is choosing the best response to what the others are choosing; no one can improve by unilaterally changing. Mutual defection in the prisoner's dilemma is a Nash equilibrium, even though it is worse for everyone than mutual cooperation.

An outcome is **Pareto efficient** if there is no alternative that makes someone better off without making anyone worse off. Mutual cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma is Pareto efficient; mutual defection is not. Yet rational agents, each pursuing their own interests, can get stuck in Pareto-inefficient equilibria.

This is the core problem of social existence: how do agents escape bad equilibria? How does cooperation emerge among beings who each have their own goals?

### Historical and Philosophical Context

Game theory was developed by John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern, with the equilibrium concept introduced by John Nash in 1950. The prisoner's dilemma was formalized by Flood and Dresher at RAND and became the canonical model for studying cooperation. Robert Axelrod's tournaments with the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma demonstrated how cooperative strategies like Tit-for-Tat can succeed.
