## Chapter 8: What Utility Is Not

Utility is easily misunderstood. It is not money—money is one thing I might care about, but utility represents whatever I actually care about, all things considered. If gaining $1,000 matters more to me when I am poor than when I am rich, then my utility function captures that; it is not the same as my bank balance.

Utility is not happiness in the moment. It represents my preferences across all considerations—future experiences, other people's welfare, abstract values, achievements, anything I care about. If I prefer a meaningful struggle to a pleasant stupor, that preference is reflected in my utility.

The utility function is a **representation**, not a cause. I do not have a utility function inscribed in my brain that generates my preferences. Rather, I have preferences, and if they satisfy the axioms, they *can be represented* by a utility function. The preferences are fundamental; the function is a convenient mathematical summary. Moreover, the function is not unique: any positive scaling and shifting of the function represents the same preferences. There is no "zero point" of utility and no absolute scale.

The axioms are the load-bearing structure. If someone asks "why maximize expected utility?" the answer is: because that is what coherent preference *is*, given the axioms. Expected utility maximization is a consequence of the axioms, not an independent requirement.

Humans often violate these axioms. This is well-documented. But this does not show the axioms are wrong as a normative standard—it may simply show we are imperfect. The framework describes coherent preference, not actual human psychology. That said, I acknowledge the axioms are commitments. If someone rejects one of them and accepts the consequences, I cannot prove them irrational by some deeper standard. I can only show what follows from the commitments I have made.

I now have a framework for forming beliefs and making decisions. But so far, I have only considered myself facing the world alone. What happens when the world contains other agents?

### Historical and Philosophical Context

The Allais paradox (1953) showed that human choices systematically violate the independence axiom. Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory (1979) documented further deviations. The debate continues about whether these findings refute expected utility as a normative standard or merely show that humans are imperfect reasoners. Some philosophers argue that certain axioms (particularly independence) should not be accepted even as normative requirements.
