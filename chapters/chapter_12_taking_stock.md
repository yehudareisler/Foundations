# Part VI: Living With Uncertainty

## Chapter 12: Taking Stock

What have I established?

The structure of consistent reasoning is certain. Given the axioms and rules of inference I have committed to, certain conclusions follow necessarily. The probability axioms follow from the commitment to avoid exploitable beliefs. Bayes' theorem follows from the probability axioms. Expected utility maximization follows from the commitment to coherent preferences. These are mathematical truths about what consistency requires, not claims about the world.

Beyond these structural certainties, I have made pragmatic commitments. I treat the external world as real and other minds as real—not because I have proven these, but because the models that posit them are useful for achieving my goals. I also find myself with extended goals, which orients me toward the future and gives me reason to care about consequences beyond the present moment.

Everything else is genuine uncertainty. The details of the world, the contents of other minds, what will happen tomorrow, whether my priors are well-chosen—here I have beliefs, not certainty. I update on evidence. I act despite uncertainty.

This is the human situation. I wake up. I find myself experiencing. I have goals. I cannot prove much about the world, but I must act in it. The framework tells me how to act consistently: form probabilistic beliefs, update them with evidence, maximize expected utility, consider strategic interaction with other agents.

### Historical and Philosophical Context

The distinction between certainty about the structure of reasoning and uncertainty about the world echoes Kant's distinction between the *a priori* (known independently of experience) and the *a posteriori* (known through experience). The overall stance—acting rationally under uncertainty—is developed in the Bayesian decision-theoretic tradition associated with Ramsey, Savage, and Jaynes.
