# Part V: Other Minds

## Chapter 9: Other Agents

Some patterns in my experience are best modeled as **agents**—entities with their own beliefs, goals, and decision procedures.

Certain clusters of experience—the sight of a human body, the sound of speech, patterns of responsive behavior—are predicted much better by attributing mental states than by any simpler model. When I treat the pattern as having goals and making choices, I predict its behavior well. When I treat it as a rock or a weather system, I predict poorly.

As with the external world, I cannot prove other minds exist. But also as with the external world, the model is vastly more useful than the alternatives. So I make another **pragmatic commitment**: I will treat other agents as real. They have beliefs, preferences, and decision procedures. They are, in a relevant sense, like me.

This changes everything. If there are other agents, my outcomes depend not only on my actions and the state of the world, but on *their* actions. And their actions depend on their beliefs, including their beliefs about my actions. I am no longer optimizing against nature. I am in **strategic interaction**. What does this imply for how I should act?

### Historical and Philosophical Context

The problem of other minds—how we can know that other beings have experiences—was articulated by Descartes and remains unresolved in philosophy. The argument from analogy (others behave like me, so they probably have minds like me) and inference to best explanation (minds best explain the behavior I observe) are the standard responses.
